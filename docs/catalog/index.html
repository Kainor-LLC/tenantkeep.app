Identity and privileged access (56)

IDCheckSeverityLicenceWhat it means
ID-001Global Administrator count between two and fiveHighnoneEvery Global Administrator is a full tenant takeover away from any phishing kit or malware on that person's machine. Microsoft recommends fewer than five, and two is the floor because the break-glass accounts hold the role. Counts above that usually come from convenience assignments that nobody revisited.
ID-002No permanent privileged assignments outside break-glassHighP2A standing privileged role is a standing target. Eligible assignments mean the role exists only for the hours it is activated, every activation is logged with a reason, and an attacker who lands in the account outside that window gets a normal user. This is what Entra ID P2 is for.
ID-003No administrators synced from on-premises ADHighnoneA synced account's password and status are controlled from on-premises Active Directory. If a domain controller falls, every synced administrator falls with it, and the attacker reaches the cloud without touching it. Microsoft's guidance for protecting Microsoft 365 from on-premises attacks starts with cloud-only privileged accounts.
ID-004Administrator accounts are unlicensed, separate identitiesMediumnoneAn administrator who reads mail and browses with the same account exposes the role to every phishing message and drive-by download. A separate, unlicensed admin account has no mailbox to phish and no Office session to hijack. Licenses on it are the tell that it doubles as a daily account.
ID-005Break-glass accounts exist, are cloud-only and enabledHighnoneWhen MFA, federation or a policy misfire locks every administrator out, the break-glass accounts are the only way back in. They must be cloud-only so an on-premises outage cannot take them, enabled so they work, and excluded from blocking policies so the lockout does not apply to them.
ID-006PIM activation settings on the top rolesHighP2Eligible assignments only help if activation is harder than a click. MFA on activation stops a stolen session from elevating, a duration cap limits how long an activated role lives, and approval on Global Administrator puts a second person between an attacker and the tenant.
ID-007No service principal holds Global AdministratorHighnoneA service principal with a directory role is an administrator with no MFA, no sign-in frequency and usually a secret somewhere in a pipeline. Global Administrator on one of them is the most direct path from a leaked secret to full tenant control.
ID-008User consent limited to admin consent or verified low-impact appsHighnoneConsent phishing asks a user to approve an app that reads their mail and files. If users can consent to any permission, one click gives the attacker a persistent OAuth grant that survives password changes and MFA. Restricting consent closes the door; the workflow keeps legitimate requests moving.
ID-009Admin consent workflow enabled with reviewersMediumnoneOnce user consent is restricted, users need a way to ask. Without the workflow they ask by email or not at all, and administrators grant tenant-wide consent to get them unblocked, which is worse than the user consent that was removed.
ID-010No expired or soon-to-expire application credentialsMediumnoneAn expired credential on a live integration is an outage waiting for the next token refresh. Expired credentials left in place also hide which ones are actually in use. Thirty days is the window in which rotation can be scheduled rather than done in an incident.
ID-011High-privilege application permissions justifiedHighnoneAn application permission applies to every mailbox, site or user in the tenant with no user present. Directory.ReadWrite.All or Mail.ReadWrite on a forgotten app is a complete data exfiltration path that bypasses Conditional Access entirely. Each one should have a name next to it.
ID-012No guests inactive beyond the thresholdMediumP1A guest who has not signed in for months still holds whatever was shared with them, under an identity another organisation controls. Inactive guests are the longest-lived access in most tenants and the least looked at.
ID-013Cross-tenant inbound access reviewed, not default-openMediumnoneThe default cross-tenant settings allow any external user to be invited to any application. That is fine for an organisation that has decided it, and a gap for one that never looked. The check fails on the untouched default so the decision gets made.
ID-014Sign-in and audit logs exported to Log Analytics or a SIEMHighnoneEntra keeps sign-in logs for seven to thirty days depending on license. An incident discovered later than that has no evidence to investigate. Exporting the logs is a one-time setting and the difference between knowing what happened and guessing.
ID-015No high-risk users left unaddressedHighP2A user flagged high risk has leaked credentials or attacker-pattern sign-ins. A week without action means either nobody is watching the report or the user-risk policy is missing. Either way the account is still usable by whoever triggered the detection.
ID-016No sign-ins over legacy protocols in the sign-in logHighP1Legacy protocols send a password with every request and cannot ask for MFA, so a guessed or leaked password is enough. Password spraying aims at them for that reason. The sign-in log shows who still depends on them, which is the list to fix before blocking.
ID-017Cloud passwords do not expire on a scheduleMediumnoneNIST SP 800-63B and Microsoft both advise against periodic password changes: people respond with predictable variations (Spring2026!, Summer2026!) that attackers guess first. Expiry adds help desk calls and no protection once MFA is in place; a change belongs after a suspected compromise.
ID-018Custom banned password list in useMediumP1NIST SP 800-63B asks for new passwords to be checked against words likely to be guessed. Microsoft's global list covers common passwords; only the custom list knows the organisation's own name, products and places, which are the first words a password spray tries.
ID-019Password protection enforced on-premisesMediumP1With synced identities the password is set in Active Directory, so the cloud banned list only protects what changes in the cloud. The agents carry the same list to the domain controllers; in Audit mode they only log.
ID-020Guests cannot invite other guestsHighnoneWhen guests can invite guests, one compromised or careless partner account can bring in anyone, and nobody in the organisation approved them. Keeping invitations with members or named inviters keeps a person accountable for every guest.
ID-021Guest access limited to their own objectsHighnoneA guest with the same directory access as members can list every user, group and device, which is reconnaissance for phishing and a map of the organisation. Restricted access is CISA's baseline and breaks nothing guests normally do.
ID-022Outbound guest access to other tenants reviewedMediumnoneBy default every user can accept a guest invitation to any other Microsoft 365 tenant and take the organisation's files into it under their work identity. Allowing only known partners closes a quiet exfiltration route that data loss prevention does not watch.
ID-023Admin command-line tools open only to the people who need themMediumnoneAny member can sign in to Graph PowerShell, Graph Explorer, the Azure CLI or the Exchange, SharePoint and Teams shells and read whatever their account can see: the whole directory, groups, devices and much of the configuration. Attackers with a stolen password or token use exactly these tools, because they are trusted Microsoft apps with consent already in place. Limiting them to the people who need them removes a ready-made reconnaissance kit from every ordinary account.
ID-024No application holds Graph permissions it has stopped usingMediumP1An application permission works with no user present, for as long as the application has a credential. An application that has not signed in for months and still holds permissions on Microsoft Graph is access nobody is watching: its secret can be in an old script, a retired server or a former supplier's hands. Applications made in the last 90 days are too new to judge and are left out.
ID-025Only administrators can register applicationsMediumnoneAny user who can register an app can create one with a secret, give it a convincing name and ask colleagues to consent to it. Forgotten test apps with credentials start the same way. Limiting registration to a role keeps the list of apps to ones somebody answers for.
ID-026Handing out a privileged role alerts someoneMediumP2An attacker who reaches one administrator account gives a second account a role so the access survives a password reset. The alert on a new assignment is how that is noticed the same day. Role by role, the alert can be switched off, and then a new Global Administrator appears without a word.
ID-027Activating a privileged role alerts someoneMediumP2Just-in-time roles only help if somebody sees the activations. An activation of Global Administrator at three in the morning from an account that never does that is the clearest signal PIM gives, and it is an email nobody receives when the alert has no recipient.
ID-028New client secrets are refused on applicationsMediumnoneA client secret is a password for an application: it sits in a configuration file, a pipeline variable or a script, and it works from anywhere with no MFA. An attacker with rights over an application adds a secret of their own and keeps the access after the person they phished changes a password. Refusing new secrets closes both.
ID-029Application secrets and certificates have a maximum lifetimeMediumnoneThe portal offers two years for a secret and lets a script ask for a hundred. A credential nobody has to renew is one nobody remembers, and it keeps working long after the person who made it has left. A maximum lifetime turns every credential into something that is looked at again.
ID-030Only administrators can create tenantsMediumnoneAnyone in the directory can create a new Entra tenant and is its Global Administrator from the first minute. That tenant is outside your policies, your logs and your offboarding, and it is a quiet place to move company data to or to run an app registration from.
ID-031Only administrators can create security groupsLownoneSecurity groups are what access is granted to: applications, SharePoint sites, licences, exclusions from a policy. A group anyone can create, with any name, is one that ends up in a permission list because it sounded official.
ID-032Creating Microsoft 365 groups is limited to the people you chooseLowP1Every Microsoft 365 group brings a mailbox, a SharePoint site and often a team, each with its own sharing. When everyone can create them, the tenant fills with groups nobody owns and sites nobody reviews, which is where overshared files and forgotten guests live.
ID-033Nobody gets an account with an email address aloneMediumnoneWith these two settings on, anyone who can receive mail at one of your domains can create themselves an account in your directory, by signing up for a self-service product or by proving they own the address. A shared mailbox, a forgotten alias or a contractor's forwarded address is enough, and the account never went through onboarding.
ID-034Administrators cannot reset their own password by self-serviceLownoneSelf-service reset for an administrator is a second way into the most powerful accounts in the tenant, and it is guarded by a phone number and a mailbox, not by the sign-in rules you wrote for administrators. Sending those resets through another administrator puts a person in the path.
ID-035Risky consent requests go to an administratorMediumnoneConsent phishing sends a real Microsoft consent screen for the attacker's app. Microsoft marks a request as risky when the publisher is unverified and the app asks for more than the basics, and by default sends it to an administrator. With that switched off, the person the attacker picked is the only reviewer.
ID-036Group owners cannot consent to apps for their groupsLownoneAn owner of a team can let an app read that team's messages and files without anyone else seeing the request. Owners are picked for running a project, not for judging what an app will do with a year of conversation.
ID-037Admin consent requests reach a reviewer and expireLownoneThe workflow replaces a person approving an app on their own with a request to a reviewer. If the reviewer is never told, the request sits until it lapses, and the person who asked finds another way to get the app.
ID-038Smart lockout stops password guessing within ten triesMediumnoneSmart lockout is what makes guessing a password slow: after so many wrong tries from an unfamiliar place the account refuses further guesses for a while. A threshold raised to stop help desk calls, or a lockout shortened to seconds, gives an attacker thousands of guesses a day at every account.
ID-039Guests cannot own Microsoft 365 groupsLownoneThe owner of a group decides who is in it, and with it who reads its mailbox, its site and its team. A guest as owner means somebody outside the organisation is adding and removing people from your data, under their own employer's security, not yours.
ID-040The MSOnline PowerShell module is blockedLownoneMSOnline is the oldest way to script the directory, and any signed-in user could use it to list every account, group and role holder in the tenant. Microsoft has retired the module; the setting closes the door for whatever old tooling still knocks on it.
ID-041People do not read their own BitLocker keys by self-serviceLownoneThe recovery key unlocks the disk without the PIN or the person. If the owner can read it from their account, so can anyone who has taken over that account and has the laptop, which is the stolen-laptop case BitLocker exists for.
ID-042Tenant restrictions block other organisations by defaultMediumnoneYour controls end at the edge of your tenant: a person can sign in to a tenant of their own from a managed laptop and move files into it. Tenant restrictions are how you say which other organisations may be signed in to from your devices. The default that ships allows everything. This check reads the default; the policy still has to be enforced on the device or the network to do anything.
ID-043App registrations sign in with certificates, not client secretsMediumnoneA client secret is a password for an application: a string that works from anywhere, gets pasted into configuration files and pipelines, and is as good as the application's permissions to whoever finds it. A certificate's private key can stay in a vault or on the machine, and a managed identity has no credential to leak. The applications listed still hold a secret.
ID-044Application certificates are valid for no longer than 180 daysLownoneA certificate that is valid for years is a credential nobody has to think about for years, including after its private key was copied. A short life forces rotation into a routine and bounds how long a stolen key works. The applications listed hold a certificate valid for longer than the limit from start to end.
ID-045Service principals hold no credentials of their ownMediumnoneAn application's secrets and certificates belong on its registration, where the portal shows them and owners look after them. A credential added to the service principal in your tenant is easy to miss, works just as well, and for an application published by someone else it lets its holder act as that application in your tenant. The signing certificate of a SAML application is not counted.
ID-046No credential has been added to a Microsoft applicationHighnoneMicrosoft's own applications, such as Exchange Online or Microsoft Graph tools, carry broad permissions in every tenant and are never meant to hold a credential of yours. Adding a secret to one of their service principals lets an attacker sign in as that application with those permissions, and it survives a password reset of every account. It is a known way of keeping access after a compromise. There is no routine reason for it.
ID-047Every AI agent and agent blueprint has an ownerMediumnoneAn owner is the technical contact for an agent: the one who can change its credentials, see what it is doing and switch it off. An agent with no owner keeps its access, and nobody is placed to notice when it is misused or no longer needed. This check counts the owners on agent identities, on agent blueprints and on the blueprints' service principals. It does not tell an owner whose account is disabled from one who is still here.
ID-048Every AI agent and agent blueprint has a sponsorLownoneAn owner keeps an agent running; a sponsor answers for it. The sponsor is who is asked whether the agent is still needed, whether its access is still right, and what to do when it misbehaves. Without one, an access review of the agent has nobody to go to and the agent outlives the project it was made for. This check counts sponsors on agent identities, agent blueprints and the blueprints' service principals.
ID-049No disabled agent is left in the directoryLownoneA disabled agent cannot sign in, but it still holds its permissions, its roles and its place in groups, and turning it back on restores all of them at once. Left for months, nobody remembers what it was for or whether the reason it was stopped still stands. This check lists agent identities and blueprints' service principals that are disabled.
ID-050No agent has lost the blueprint or the agent it belongs toMediumnoneAn agent identity is made from a blueprint, and an agent's user account is paired with an agent identity. When the parent is deleted and the child stays, what is left is an identity with access and nothing managing it: no blueprint holding its credentials and settings, or a user account with a mailbox and licences that no agent is behind. A service principal for a blueprint that another organisation publishes is not counted, since its blueprint was never in this tenant.
ID-051No AI agent holds a privileged directory roleHighnoneAn agent acts on what it reads, and what it reads can be written by an attacker: a document, an email, a web page. An agent that holds Global Administrator or another privileged role turns one planted instruction into a change to the tenant. This check looks for a privileged role held by an agent identity, by a blueprint's service principal or by an agent's user account: assigned, eligible, or through a group. A blueprint published by another organisation is named as such.
ID-052No AI agent holds a high-privilege Microsoft Graph permissionHighnoneAn application permission on Microsoft Graph works with no person signed in. The ones counted here can change the directory, its policies or who holds a role, or read every mailbox and file. Held by an agent, they are within reach of whatever the agent can be talked into doing. The check reads application permissions granted to agent identities and to blueprints' service principals, whose agents can be given what the blueprint holds. Delegated permissions are not counted.
ID-053Agent blueprints pass on only the permissions they nameMediumnoneA blueprint can hand its permissions on an API to every agent made from it. Set to all allowed, each agent gets whatever the blueprint holds on that API, and anything granted to the blueprint later as well. A permission added to the blueprint for one purpose then reaches every agent without anyone deciding that it should.
ID-054Agent blueprints that expose roles require assignmentMediumnoneA blueprint can define application roles that say what a caller may ask its agents to do. When its service principal does not require assignment, Entra issues a token for it to any user or application in the tenant, and what happens next depends on the agent checking the roles itself. With assignment required, only the callers you chose get a token at all.
ID-055Agent blueprints have no wildcard or plain-http redirect addressMediumnoneAfter a sign-in, Entra sends the result to a redirect address registered on the blueprint. An address with a wildcard matches hosts nobody meant it to, and one over plain http can be read on the way. Either lets someone else receive what was meant for the agent.
ID-056Agent blueprint credentials are current, few and short-livedMediumnoneA blueprint's credential is what lets its agents get tokens. An expired one is clutter that hides the one in use. A long-lived one works for years after it leaks. Several working at once means nobody knows which is in use. A secret kept beside a federated credential is the weaker way in, left open. This check fails a blueprint on any of the four.

Authentication methods (14)

IDCheckSeverityLicenceWhat it means
AU-001SMS and voice disabled or scoped to a migration groupHighnoneSMS and voice are the weakest second factors: SIM swaps, SS7 interception and social engineering of carriers all defeat them, and they cannot be made phishing-resistant. Leaving them enabled for all users means an attacker who needs a second factor picks the easiest one.
AU-002Authenticator shows app and location contextMediumnoneMFA fatigue attacks spam approvals until the user taps one. Number matching made the tap deliberate; showing the requesting app and the sign-in location tells the user that the request from another country for an app they are not using is not theirs.
AU-003Passkeys enabledHighnonePasskeys are the phishing-resistant method most users can actually adopt: no code to type, nothing to relay, bound to the site. If the method is disabled nothing else in the phishing-resistant story can start.
AU-004Legacy MFA and SSPR policies migratedMediumnoneWhile the migration is in progress, three policies decide which methods a user can register and the effective result is the union of them. Finishing the migration makes the authentication methods policy the one place that matters, which is what every other check here assumes.
AU-005Temporary Access Pass enabled, one-time by defaultLownoneA Temporary Access Pass is how a new user or someone with a lost phone registers a passkey without a password-only window. A reusable pass with a long lifetime is itself a credential worth stealing, so one-time use is the default to keep.
AU-006MFA registered for every memberHighnoneAn MFA policy only protects users who have a method registered; the rest are prompted to register at their next sign-in, which an attacker with the password is happy to do. The registered percentage is the real coverage number behind every MFA policy.
AU-007Passwordless-capable share of members at targetLownonePasswordless-capable users can sign in without a password at all, which removes the credential phishing steals. The number is reported here and compared against the target the organisation sets, since a sensible target depends on where the rollout is.
AU-008Every administrator has a phishing-resistant method registeredCriticalnoneThe phishing-resistant policy for administrators (CA-003) can only be enforced once every administrator has a method that satisfies it. This check is the readiness gate: who still signs in with a push notification or a code that an adversary-in-the-middle kit can relay.
AU-009No users on legacy per-user MFAMediumnonePer-user MFA predates Conditional Access and prompts on every sign-in regardless of risk, device or app. Users on it get MFA fatigue, administrators get no policy control, and it cannot require a phishing-resistant method. It is the tell of a tenant that was set up before 2019 and never revisited.
AU-010People can report an MFA prompt they did not startMediumnoneA prompt nobody asked for means somebody else has the password. Without this setting all the person can do is tap Deny, and nothing records it. With it, the report marks the account as high risk, which your risk policy and your administrators can act on within the minute.
AU-011Passkeys must prove what kind of key they areLownoneAttestation is the key stating its make and model, signed by its manufacturer. Without it the directory takes any passkey at its word, including software that only claims to be a hardware key and lets its secret be copied. For the accounts that matter most, knowing what the key is belongs to trusting it.
AU-012People can add a passkey themselvesMediumnoneWith self-service set up off, the passkey method is enabled and nobody can register one: an administrator would have to provision every key. Passkeys then stay a pilot for good. This is the switch that lets the rollout happen.
AU-013Passkeys are limited to the keys you choseMediumnoneWithout key restrictions any FIDO2 authenticator can be registered, a cheap key with poor protection for its secret or a passkey synced to a personal password manager among them. A list of the keys you accept is how you know what your phishing-resistant sign-ins actually rest on. The check counts how many kinds of key are named, not which.
AU-014Microsoft Authenticator one-time codes are offLownoneA six-digit code can be read out to a caller or typed into a fake sign-in page, which is how most MFA phishing works. The notification with number matching and location is harder to give away, and a passkey cannot be given away at all. Turning the code off removes the weakest thing Authenticator can do.

Conditional Access (31)

IDCheckSeverityLicenceWhat it means
CA-001Legacy authentication blockedCriticalP1Legacy protocols such as basic-auth IMAP, POP and SMTP cannot complete a multifactor prompt, so a password spray or a stolen password signs in without any second factor. Blocking them is the single control that removes most password-only sign-ins from the tenant. A policy in report-only mode or with a broad exclusion group leaves the door open for exactly the accounts an attacker will try.
CA-002MFA required for all usersCriticalP1A tenant-wide MFA policy is the baseline every other control assumes. Per-user MFA and security defaults cover fewer scenarios and cannot be scoped or reported on. Every account excluded from the policy is an account that signs in with a password alone, which is why the check counts a policy as passing only when its exclusions are limited to the break-glass accounts.
CA-003Phishing-resistant authentication for administrator rolesCriticalP1Push notifications and one-time codes can be relayed by an adversary-in-the-middle kit, which captures the session as soon as the administrator approves. Phishing-resistant methods bind the credential to the site, so the relayed prompt fails. Administrators are the accounts those kits are aimed at, and the roles listed here are the ones Microsoft recommends protecting first.
CA-004Risk-based policies for sign-in risk and user riskHighP2Identity Protection scores sign-ins and users on leaked credentials, anonymous networks, impossible travel and attacker infrastructure. Without a policy that acts on the score, the detections are a report nobody reads until after the incident. Requiring MFA on risky sign-ins and a password change on risky users turns the detection into an automatic response.
CA-005Compliant device required for Office 365 on Windows and macOSHighP1, IntuneA stolen password with a valid MFA approval still lands on whatever machine the attacker chooses. Requiring a compliant device ties access to a managed, patched, encrypted endpoint with endpoint protection reporting in, which is the control that keeps session theft and credential phishing from turning into data access.
CA-006No policy left in report-only modeMediumP1Report-only mode exists to measure impact before enforcing. A policy that stays in it for months protects nothing while looking like a control on the policy list. The pileup is a common finding in tenants where a rollout stalled, and it usually hides the policies that would matter most.
CA-007Exclusion groups under the size thresholdHighP1Every member of an exclusion group is outside the policy entirely. Groups created for a migration or a vendor tend to grow and never shrink, and an attacker who lands in one of those accounts faces no MFA at all. The size threshold is the point at which an exclusion stops being an exception and becomes a second, weaker policy.
CA-008No MFA bypass for trusted locationsHighP1Skipping MFA from the office network treats the network as the credential. Anyone on the wireless, a compromised device on the LAN, or a VPN session from a stolen laptop then signs in with a password alone. Zero trust guidance drops the location bypass because the perimeter is the easiest control to get behind.
CA-009Sign-in frequency and non-persistent browser for administratorsMediumP1An administrator session that persists for weeks is a token worth stealing. Limiting how long the session lives and preventing the browser from keeping it caps what a stolen cookie is worth, and it is the control that makes a token replay noticeable rather than silent.
CA-010Token protection in useMediumP1Token protection binds the sign-in token to the device it was issued to, so a session cookie copied from a phished or malware-infected machine stops working elsewhere. It closes the gap that MFA leaves open once the user has already signed in. Client support is still growing, which is why report-only with a date counts here.
CA-011Microsoft-managed policies not silently disabledMediumP1Microsoft creates managed policies in tenants for the controls it considers baseline, such as MFA for administrators and for risky sign-ins, and enables them after a notice period. Disabling one without an equivalent policy removes a control the tenant was about to gain, and the state is easy to miss because the policy stays on the list.
CA-012Break-glass accounts excluded from every blocking policyHighP1A blocking policy that applies to the break-glass accounts can lock every administrator out of the tenant at once, typically during the incident the policy was written for. A policy misfire, an MFA outage or a mistaken location change then has no way back in. Excluding the emergency accounts from every Block is what keeps Conditional Access recoverable.
CA-013No policy references a deleted objectHighP1When a group, user or named location a policy references is deleted, the policy keeps the orphaned id and silently stops covering or excluding whoever it pointed at. The portal shows nothing wrong. The result is a policy that reads correctly and does something different, which is the worst kind of drift.
CA-014No policy left disabled past the thresholdLowP1Disabled policies accumulate from pilots and emergencies and make the policy list hard to read, which is how a reviewer misses the one that matters. A disabled policy can also be re-enabled by mistake with stale assignments. Either it is needed, and should be enabled, or it is not, and should go.
CA-015Policy names follow the naming conventionLowP1A naming convention is what lets a reviewer read a policy list and know what each policy is for without opening it. Tenants whose policies are named by whoever created them tend to have duplicates and gaps nobody noticed. With a pattern in the profile the check enforces it; without one it looks for a prefix-and-number structure across the names, and free-text names are the finding.
CA-016Device code sign-in blockedMediumP1Device code phishing is a sign-in on the attacker's behalf: the attacker starts a device code sign-in with a trusted app, sends the code to a user as an urgent request, and the user signs in with MFA on the real Microsoft page, handing the attacker a token. MFA does not stop it because the user completes MFA. Few organisations need device code sign-in at all, so blocking it closes the attack for almost no cost.
CA-017Registering a sign-in method is protected by Conditional AccessMediumP1The page where a person adds an authenticator is the page an attacker wants most. With a stolen password and no rule on registration, the attacker registers their own phone on an account that has not set up MFA yet, and from then on passes every MFA prompt. A policy on that one action closes it.
CA-018Authentication transfer blockedLowP1Authentication transfer hands a signed-in session from a computer to a phone through a QR code. It skips the checks a fresh sign-in on the phone would meet, the device check among them, and a person can be talked into scanning a code that signs an attacker's phone in. Few organisations depend on it.
CA-019No policy asks for the retired approved client app grantMediumP1Microsoft retired the approved client app grant and replaced it with the app protection policy grant. A policy that still asks for the old control is a rule Microsoft no longer enforces as written, so the phones it was meant to hold to managed apps are not held to anything.
CA-020User risk and sign-in risk are in separate policiesMediumP2A Conditional Access policy applies only when every one of its conditions is met. With user risk and sign-in risk in the same policy, it acts only on a risky sign-in by someone already marked at risk. A leaked password with a normal-looking sign-in, or an odd sign-in by a clean account, passes straight through.
CA-021Unknown device platforms are blockedLowP1Your device rules are written per platform: compliant Windows, managed iPhones. A platform nobody wrote a rule for meets none of them. An attacker's tooling can claim to be any platform it likes, and the ones with no rule are the ones it picks.
CA-022Unmanaged devices get a browser session that endsMediumP1A browser on a home or hotel computer keeps a session for weeks unless told otherwise, and the next person at that keyboard, or malware that copies the cookie, has the mailbox. On a device you manage you can protect the session; on one you do not, the only control is to make it short.
CA-023Office 365 limits what an unmanaged device can doLowP1Blocking unmanaged devices outright is more than many organisations can do. This is the middle setting: people can still read and edit in a browser, and the file never lands on a disk you cannot wipe. It takes effect only when both halves are set, the policy here and the unmanaged devices setting in SharePoint.
CA-024Every enabled policy targets somethingMediumP1A policy with no application, no user action and no authentication context applies to nothing. It still shows as On in the list, with a name that says what it protects, and everyone who reads the list believes it. It is usually what is left after an application was removed from the tenant.
CA-025Guests sign in again within a dayMediumP1A guest's account lives in another organisation, which you cannot see: you do not know when they left it, or when their password was last stolen. A short session is the one control on your side that limits how long a token taken from a guest stays useful. This check looks for an enabled policy that reaches guests and sets a sign-in frequency within the threshold.
CA-026Risky workload identities are blockedMediumWorkloadIDApplications sign in with secrets and certificates that leak into code, logs and build systems, and no person is there to notice an odd sign-in. ID Protection rates service principals for risk as it does users. Without a policy that acts on the rating, a leaked credential keeps working after Microsoft has flagged it.
CA-027Workload identities are held to the networks they call fromLowWorkloadIDA secret copied from a pipeline is used from the thief's network, not yours. A location policy for service principals makes the stolen copy worthless there. It applies to single-tenant applications you own, and it needs you to know where each one runs, which is worth knowing anyway.
CA-028A trusted named location is definedLowP1ID Protection rates a sign-in partly by where it comes from, and it knows your offices only if you tell it. With a trusted location defined, a sign-in from the office is not flagged as unfamiliar and one from elsewhere stands out more. The same location lets a policy ask more of an administrator who is not on a network you know.
